Sand, heat and a bath of molten tin

How the float process turned glassmaking into a river that never stops.

By Mara Ellison · 9 min read
In this story

Almost every window you have looked through was made by floating. Molten glass, mostly silica sand with soda ash and limestone, pours from the furnace onto a shallow bath of molten tin. Glass is lighter than tin, so it spreads across the metal and levels itself: flat, and parallel on both faces, with no grinding or polishing. The trick is called the float processfj.

Before it, plate glass was cast or rolled, then ground and polished on both sides: slow, costly work that wore away part of every sheet. The method developed at Pilkingtonfk in the 1950s replaced all of that with gravity and surface tension, and it now makes the great majority of the world's flat glass.

The ribbon enters the bath at around 1,100 °C and leaves at about 600 °C, stiff enough to ride on rollers without marking. From there it passes through the lehrfl, a long annealing oven, where it cools slowly so no stress is locked inside.

“Gravity does the polishing.”

A float line runs day and night for years between rebuilds. Stopping it is harder than keeping it going, so the furnace never goes cold and the ribbon is simply cut to length as it arrives: a river of glass that never stops.

The line, stage by stage
  1. Batch. Sand, soda ash, limestone and recycled cullet are weighed and mixed.
  2. Furnace. The batch melts at about 1,500 °C.
  3. Tin bath. The ribbon floats, spreads and flattens.
  4. Lehr. Slow cooling relieves internal stress.
  5. Cutting. The ribbon is scored and snapped to size.
