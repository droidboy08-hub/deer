Float glass

Field Notes · 6 min read

Most flat glass made today is float glass. Molten glass is poured onto a bath of molten tin, where surface tension pulls it into a sheet of even thickness. The ribbon leaves the bath at about 600 °C and moves through the annealing lehr, where it cools slowly enough that no stress is locked in.

The process was developed by Pilkington in the 1950s. Before it, plate glass had to be ground and polished on both faces, which wasted a fifth of the glass and most of a day. Float glass came off the line with a fire-polished finish that needed nothing more.

Thickness is set by the speed of the line and by top rollers that push or pull at the edges of the ribbon. Slow it down and the glass piles up thicker; speed it up and it thins, down to about 0.4 mm for display glass. The tin side of a sheet still carries a trace of tin, which glows under ultraviolet light.

Coatings are added on the line while the glass is still hot: low-emissivity layers for windows, conductive oxides for touch screens, and the thin metal stacks that make a mirror. Each is a few hundred nanometres thick, and each changes what the glass does with light.

Cutting happens at the cold end. A wheel scores the sheet, a bar snaps it, and the offcuts go back into the furnace as cullet, which melts at a lower temperature than fresh batch and saves energy.

Toughened glass is float glass heated again and quenched with air, so that its faces are in compression and its core in tension. It is four or five times as strong, and when it breaks it breaks into small blunt pieces. It cannot be cut afterwards: the whole sheet would shatter.
